    \title{Perovskite Solar Cell Performance Prediction with LLM-Derived Feature Augmentation under Leakage-Aware DOI Evaluation}

    \author[1]{Sunraj Patel}
    \author[1]{Shikhar Misra\corref{cor1}}

    \cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
    \ead{shikharm@iitk.ac.in}

    \address[1]{Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, India 208016}


    \begin{abstract}
        Machine-learning (ML) models for perovskite solar cell (PSC) performance are increasingly trained on literature-derived databases such as the Perovskite Database Project (PDP), yet the PDP studies that describe their partitioning use record-level splits, even though a single publication often contributes many closely related device records. Here we build a power conversion efficiency (PCE) prediction framework on 41,107 PDP device records from 7,326 publications and evaluate it on unseen publications. Three nested feature tiers are compared: PDP-derived device, processing and composition features ($t_1$); $t_1$ plus curated material-property descriptors ($t_2$); and $t_2$ plus 36 binary recipe flags (additive and precursor identities, electron- and hole-transport-layer dopants, process route and device context) built deterministically from full-text information extracted with a locally served large language model (LLM) ($t_3$), available for 36.6\% of records. All tiers are trained with one fixed CatBoost configuration, a DOI-disjoint validation set for early stopping, and no test-based selection. On reserved publications scored once for this representation (14,125 records from 2,619 publications; they had, however, appeared in earlier experiments with other feature representations), $R^2$ rose from 0.371 ($t_1$) to 0.403 ($t_2$) and 0.406 ($t_3$) on all records, and from 0.318 to 0.359 and 0.366 on the 5,460 records (1,005 publications) whose publications have LLM-derived features. The material-property step is clear (paired $\Delta R^2 = +0.042$, 95\% interval $+0.021$ to $+0.063$, on LLM-covered records; $+0.032$, $+0.021$ to $+0.044$, on all records), whereas the recipe-flag step is small and not statistically established ($\Delta R^2 = +0.0071$, $-0.0007$ to $+0.0157$, covered; $+0.0025$, $-0.0011$ to $+0.0062$, all records), and it depends on the CatBoost configuration. Under repeated DOI-grouped holdouts the pooled $R^2$ of $t_1$ and $t_2$ is $0.368 \pm 0.024$ and $0.408 \pm 0.032$ (MAE 2.95 percentage points for $t_2$; mean $\pm$ s.d. over five overlapping holdouts); a material-identity control recovers essentially all of the $t_1 \rightarrow t_2$ gain, so it reflects knowing which materials are present. Under matched features and budgets, record-level random splitting yields a converged CatBoost $R^2$ of 0.59--0.62, i.e.\ 0.21--0.22 higher than DOI-grouped evaluation, while a ridge model shows a gap of only 0.06; 69\% of PCE variance lies between publications. DOI-grouped evaluation therefore gives a more defensible estimate of performance on new publications; lead-author grouping changes little (0.394 for $t_2$) and forward-in-time evaluation lowers it to 0.345; no external validation was possible.
    \end{abstract}

% ---------------- INTRODUCTION ----------------
\section{Introduction}

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are promising photovoltaic devices whose performance depends on the absorber material, device architecture, and fabrication conditions. A typical planar PSC consists of five functional layers: a substrate, an electron transport layer (ETL), a perovskite absorber, a hole transport layer (HTL), and a metal electrode. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:psc_structures}, two widely used configurations are n-i-p and p-i-n, which differ in the relative positions of the ETL and HTL \cite{jung2015perovskite,zuo2016advances}. Under illumination, the perovskite absorber generates charge carriers, while the ETL and HTL selectively transport electrons and holes, respectively, towards the electrodes. Charge extraction and recombination, together with the properties of the constituent materials, influence the resulting photovoltaic performance \cite{jung2015perovskite,zuo2016advances}.

Power conversion efficiency (PCE) is the principal metric of PSC performance and represents the fraction of incident optical power converted into electrical power. The fill factor (FF) characterizes the shape of the current--voltage curve as the ratio of the maximum output power to the product of the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current. These quantities are expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PCE} =
    \frac{V_{\mathrm{OC}}J_{\mathrm{SC}}\mathrm{FF}}{P_{\mathrm{in}}}
    \times 100\%,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FF} =
    \frac{V_{\mathrm{MPP}}J_{\mathrm{MPP}}}
    {V_{\mathrm{OC}}J_{\mathrm{SC}}},
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{OC}}$ and $J_{\mathrm{SC}}$ denote the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current density, respectively; $V_{\mathrm{MPP}}$ and $J_{\mathrm{MPP}}$ are the voltage and current density at the maximum power point; and $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the incident optical power density \cite{zuo2016advances}. The dependence of PCE on multiple interacting material, architectural, and processing variables makes systematic device optimization challenging. Machine learning (ML) offers a data-driven approach to identifying these relationships and exploring combinations of experimentally reported parameters that would be difficult to investigate exhaustively through conventional trial-and-error approaches \cite{odabasi2019performance,bansal2023machine,li2019predictions,liu2022machine,gok2022predicting}.

The availability of large experimental databases has enabled ML models to be developed using combinations of material, device, and process information. The Perovskite Database Project (PDP) provides an open-access collection of PSC data covering material compositions, device structures, fabrication conditions, and photovoltaic characteristics \cite{jacobsson2022open}. Roberts et al. developed an open-source ML pipeline using the PDP and evaluated XGBoost, artificial neural networks, and Gaussian process regression for predicting short-circuit current density. Their XGBoost model achieved a test $R^2$ of 0.35 and an RMSE of $3.58$ mA cm$^{-2}$, while their analysis highlighted the importance of data cleaning, feature encoding, and reproducible workflows \cite{roberts2024machine}. Other device-level studies have similarly examined the influence of descriptor selection and dataset characteristics on predictive performance \cite{sala2023assessing}.

Fukasawa et al. examined the representation of material and process information specifically for PCE prediction using the PDP \cite{fukasawa2024effectiveness}. Starting with 42,459 records, they curated the dataset to obtain 36,937 records with 248 material- and process-related variables. Their results demonstrated that incorporating process information improved prediction compared with using perovskite composition alone. Under an 8:2 train--test split (the article does not describe grouping by publication), their best feature representation achieved a test $R^2$ of 0.7177; however, evaluation on 294 newly registered records yielded an $R^2$ of 0.45 and an MAE of 3.20\% \cite{fukasawa2024effectiveness}. They also identified substantial data degeneracy, with 6,446 unique degenerate points corresponding to 20,695 feature vectors, indicating that distinct experimental records can have identical feature representations despite exhibiting different PCE values.

The distinction between interpolation within an existing dataset and generalization to new groups of observations is also recognized in the broader materials-informatics and machine-learning literature. Meredig et al. introduced leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation to examine how evaluation strategies affect estimates of model performance for materials discovery, emphasizing that conventional random validation may not adequately represent extrapolative use cases \cite{meredig2018cluster}. More broadly, Kapoor and Narayanan documented data leakage as a recurring source of overoptimistic findings and reproducibility problems across scientific machine-learning applications \cite{kapoor2023leakage}. These studies establish that evaluation should reflect the intended generalization setting and the dependence structure of the data. In literature-compiled PSC datasets, publication DOI provides a practical grouping variable for assessing performance on papers excluded from training. Our work applies this principle to PCE prediction and quantifies how the estimated performance and feature-tier comparisons change when publication-level overlap is removed.

The literature-derived nature of PSC databases raises a further concern about model evaluation. Multiple devices reported in the same publication may share material selections, fabrication procedures, device architectures, and measurement conditions, making record-level independence an assumption that requires scrutiny. Roberts et al.\ released an open-source cleaning and modelling pipeline for the Perovskite Database Project (PDP) with short-circuit current density as the target; repeated device configurations that arose during their feature reduction were replaced by a single pseudo-target, and the models were evaluated on an 80/10/10 train/validation/test split \cite{roberts2024machine}. Fukasawa et al. reported a reduction in predictive performance from \(R^2=0.7177\) under their conventional evaluation to \(R^2=0.45\) with an MAE of \(3.20\) percentage points on 294 newly registered records \cite{fukasawa2024effectiveness}. This result provides prior evidence that performance estimates can be sensitive to the evaluation set and the distribution of newly reported devices. However, the newly registered-record evaluation was relatively small and was not presented as a systematic DOI-grouped cross-validation protocol. Of the PDP-based studies we examined, the two that describe their partitioning use record-level splits \cite{roberts2024machine,fukasawa2024effectiveness}, and a further PCE study does not state its splitting procedure \cite{hussain2023leveraging}. Group-aware, cluster-based, forward and time-split validation are established in materials informatics, cheminformatics and ecology \cite{meredig2018cluster,stanev2018superconductor,kauwe2020extraordinary,xiong2020forward,sheridan2013timesplit,roberts2017crossvalidation}; the contribution here is narrower. To our knowledge, no earlier study compares record-level and DOI-grouped evaluation on the same PDP export under matched features, preprocessing and model settings (our literature search was not exhaustive), and we do so here. Such evaluation prevents records sharing a DOI from appearing in both training and test partitions, providing a direct assessment of generalization to publications excluded from training.


In this work, we develop a literature- and material-augmented ML framework for predicting PSC PCE from the PDP, and evaluate it at the publication level. The contributions are twofold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Publication-level evaluation.} We compare record-level random splitting with repeated DOI-grouped holdouts under matched feature tiers, models, preprocessing and budgets, with DOI normalisation, an explicit treatment of records lacking a DOI, and a DOI-disjoint validation set so that no model selection touches the test publications. We quantify the gap for a flexible (CatBoost) and a linear (ridge) model, relate it to the within-publication structure of the data, and add lead-author and forward-in-time protocols.
    \item \textbf{Three nested feature tiers, including LLM-derived recipe flags.} We combine PDP device and fabrication variables with composition-derived descriptors ($t_1$), curated layer-resolved material properties ($t_2$), and 36 binary recipe flags derived from LLM-extracted full-text information for 2,731 publications ($t_3$). The three tiers are compared on identical reserved publications and test records with one fixed CatBoost configuration. Material-property descriptors give a measurable gain on unseen publications, which a material-identity control attributes to knowing which materials are used; the recipe flags give a small additional gain whose interval includes zero, and coverage is limited to 36.6\% of records.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data Preparation}

\subsubsection{Dataset Description}

The Perovskite Database Project (PDP) is an open-access repository of experimentally reported perovskite solar cell (PSC) data, encompassing material compositions, device architectures, fabrication procedures, and photovoltaic characteristics \cite{jacobsson2022open}. The database export used in this study contained 43,398 device records, associated with 7,499 unique DOIs, and 410 columns. These records comprise heterogeneous data types, including categorical descriptions of functional layers, perovskite compositions, numerical device parameters, and fabrication-related variables. Previous PDP-based studies have demonstrated that appropriate data selection, cleaning, and representation are important for developing predictive models from these heterogeneous records \cite{roberts2024machine,fukasawa2024effectiveness}.

Five fields were initially extracted to construct the layer-resolved material representation: \texttt{ETL\_stack\_sequence}, \texttt{HTL\_stack\_sequence}, \texttt{Perovskite\_composition\_short\_form}, \texttt{JV\_default\_PCE}, and \texttt{Cell\_architecture}. These fields describe the electron transport layer (ETL), hole transport layer (HTL), perovskite composition, experimentally reported power conversion efficiency (PCE), and device architecture, respectively. The PCE field was renamed \texttt{final\_pce} for consistency throughout the processing pipeline, while the architecture field was retained for pooled modelling and architecture-specific analysis. This five-column extract was an intermediate representation for material processing rather than the complete predictor matrix; additional device and fabrication variables were retained from the original database for subsequent feature construction. Publication metadata, including DOI identifiers and author information, were excluded from the predictive feature set, while DOI identifiers were retained separately for linking records to the literature corpus and defining publication-level data partitions.

\subsubsection{Layer-Stack Parsing and Material Catalogue}

The material-stack descriptions in the PDP are stored as strings containing multiple materials or sequential layers, with vertical bars (\texttt{|}) delimiting successive entries. These strings were parsed into position-indexed fields, such as \texttt{ETL\_1}, \texttt{HTL\_1}, and \texttt{Perovskite\_1}, making the constituent materials independently accessible. The resulting representation accommodated up to seven ETL entries, three HTL entries, and five perovskite-layer entries. Where an entry contained multiple semicolon-separated materials, the individual materials were expanded into separate fields while preserving the recorded stack order. Material identifiers were also normalized by consolidating selected naming variants into common identifiers, facilitating consistent matching across device records.

A catalogue of unique standardized material identifiers was subsequently constructed from the parsed ETL, HTL, and perovskite fields. The catalogue contained 2,817 unique material identifiers, and the occurrence frequency of each identifier was calculated by counting its appearances across the parsed stack fields. This catalogue provided a common reference for material-level property retrieval and the construction of layer-specific descriptors (Section~\ref{sec:matprop}). The parsed identifiers were used for property lookup and were not themselves supplied to the models as categorical predictors.

\subsection{Literature Corpus Construction}

\subsubsection{DOI Identification and Full-Text Retrieval}

To enrich the structured device database with information from the original experimental literature, publication identifiers were extracted from the \texttt{Ref\_DOI\_number} field of the PDP. In total, 7,499 unique DOI strings were identified across the 43,398 device records (7,326 publications plus 232 records without a DOI after the record filtering described in Section~\ref{sec:integration}). These identifiers were used to drive an automated full-text retrieval workflow, with retrieved documents associated with their corresponding DOIs to maintain traceability throughout subsequent processing. The retrieval script released with this work (\texttt{scripts/retrieve\_fulltext.py}) requests full texts only through legal routes, in order: open-access PDF locations listed by Unpaywall and OpenAlex, openly accessible publisher full-text links deposited with Crossref, and finally manual download through institutional access; each retrieval is logged with its route. We do not assert that the historical corpus of 3,299 PDFs behind the reported features was obtained exclusively through these routes; its provenance is described in the repository retrieval notes. % AUTHOR DECISION NEEDED: legacy/NOTES_retrieval_history.md records that recovered earlier retrieval scripts also used non-legal routes (Sci-Hub domains) alongside OpenAlex; decide the final provenance statement (and whether the extraction should be regenerated from a legally retrieved corpus) before submission.
Retrieved PDFs, their Markdown conversions and the raw LLM outputs are not redistributed. The retrieval stage yielded 3,299 PDF documents. As full-text availability and successful retrieval varied across publications, the resulting corpus represented the subset of identified publications for which PDF documents were obtained.

\subsubsection{PDF Conversion and LLM-Based Information Extraction}
\label{sec:extraction}

The retrieved PDF documents were converted individually into Markdown using OpenDataLoader PDF, an open-source PDF parsing tool designed to produce machine-readable representations of document content and structure \cite{opendataloaderpdf}. This conversion yielded 3,141 Markdown documents from the 3,299 retrieved PDFs. The resulting files provided a text-based representation of each publication for subsequent processing by a large language model (LLM).

Each Markdown document was supplied individually to a locally served Qwen3.5-9B model (OpenAI-compatible vLLM endpoint, model alias \texttt{qwen35}, temperature 0.1, at most 16,384 output tokens; the exact checkpoint and quantisation are not recorded in the project files) using a predefined system prompt (reproduced in the Supplementary Information) and a structured JSON schema to guide consistent information extraction. The recovered extraction script is zero-shot; whether additional examples were used for the 2,835 outputs cannot be verified, so no few-shot claim is made. The extraction procedure was designed to identify reported materials and their functional classifications, together with available material properties, units, uncertainties, and measurement methods. Fabrication-related information, including deposition techniques, spin-coating parameters, annealing conditions, and processing environments, was also extracted where reported. Photovoltaic performance information was collected separately, with champion and average performance values distinguished where possible. The resulting structured outputs retained their association with the source DOI for subsequent paper-level feature construction.

The individual extraction outputs were consolidated into a combined JSON representation and subsequently organized into DOI-indexed JSON files. This process yielded 2,835 structured paper records. These records served as the intermediate representation for downstream feature construction, in which the extracted information was normalized and converted into a consistent set of publication-level descriptors for integration with the device-level database.

No hand-annotated reference set was available, so a true extraction precision/recall and hallucination rate were \emph{not} measured. As a proxy we compared the extracted features with the independently curated PDP entries for the same publications (\texttt{results/extraction\_validation.json}). For the numerical parameters, the paper-level value agreed with the median PDP device value within 10\% for 59--85\% of publications that have both (Spearman 0.44--0.90; perovskite thickness 0.79, $n=507$; annealing temperature 0.71, $n=455$), and for the material flags the DOI-level precision/recall against the PDP stack strings were 0.94/0.82 (TiO$_2$), 0.95/0.80 (Spiro-OMeTAD), 0.82/0.87 (PEDOT), 0.85/0.83 (NiO$_x$), 0.83/0.83 (PTAA) and 0.54/0.82 (SnO$_2$), with lower precision where the PDP records only a subset of the materials a paper describes (the BCP and LiF flags cannot be assessed because the PDP stack strings rarely contain those layers). This is agreement with a second human-curated source, not accuracy against ground truth, and it was computed for the numerical parameters and material flags of an earlier feature table, not for the 36 recipe flags of $t_3$, which were not separately validated. Importantly, the extracted numerical values equal the PDP value exactly in 59--82\% of records where both exist, and they supply a value where the PDP column is empty for only 0.8--5.3\% of records, so they are largely redundant with columns already present in $t_1$. The corpus shrank from 3,299 retrieved PDFs to 3,141 Markdown documents and 2,835 structured paper records. The recipe flags built from these records ($t_3$, Section~\ref{sec:recipe}) are non-missing for 15,027 of 41,107 filtered device records (36.6\%; 2,731 publications). An earlier literature feature table built from the same 2,835 records matched 2,726 distinct DOI strings of the filtered dataset (2,699 publications after DOI normalisation) and covered 14,896 records (36.2\%). The two coverage masks therefore differ by 32 publications and 131 records; we did not investigate the cause and do not claim that the masks are identical. In both tables the features are non-missing exactly for publications with an extraction record (individual sparsely reported process parameters of the earlier table have lower coverage, 3.7--36.2\%) and, where present, are constant within a publication (\texttt{results/feature\_audit.json}, Supplementary Table~S4).

\subsubsection{Earlier DOI-Level Feature Table}

The structured JSON records were parsed to construct a tabular dataset of publication-level features for integration with the device database. An initial, broader feature representation was developed and subsequently refined to retain information relevant to PSC materials and fabrication. The feature-construction procedure used recursive traversal of nested JSON objects to retrieve property values and associated units, together with normalization of synonymous field names and material-type labels. This harmonization enabled information expressed using different terminology across publications to be mapped to consistent feature definitions.

The resulting features represented material properties and fabrication conditions reported in the source literature, including layer-specific properties, deposition parameters, annealing conditions, and available processing-environment information. Material and layer assignments were resolved using standardized material classifications and bounded pattern matching to reduce false matches between similar material names. Layer-specific descriptors were retained separately rather than averaging properties across different functional layers. In addition to these material and processing descriptors, the table includes extraction-level descriptors, such as the number of numerical values recovered from a paper, which summarize how completely a publication reports its experimental details. Bibliographic metadata, including author names, journal names, and publication year, were excluded.

The final DOI-level feature table of this earlier representation contained one row per successfully processed publication (32 base columns, 44 predictors with their missingness indicators). These features represented information extracted from the associated research paper and were therefore defined at the publication level, rather than as independent measurements for every device reported in that paper. This earlier 44-predictor representation was superseded by the recipe flags below. It is not part of $t_1$, $t_2$ or $t_3$; its ablation is reported in the Supplementary Information and it is referred to as the ``earlier literature block''.


\subsection{Feature Engineering}

\subsubsection{Composition-Derived Descriptors}

Composition-derived descriptors were constructed to represent the elemental and ionic composition of the perovskite absorber numerically. These features included the fractions of constituent ions at the A, B, and X sites and corresponding ion counts.

In addition to the site-specific composition descriptors, the feature set included composition-derived descriptors representing the average atomic mass and ionic radius of the perovskite absorber. The feature \texttt{Perovskite\_avg\_atomic\_mass} was calculated as a stoichiometric average of the atomic masses of the constituent elements. For a composition containing \(k\) constituent elements, this descriptor is defined as

\[
\overline{M}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i M_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i},
\]

where \(n_i\) is the stoichiometric coefficient of element \(i\) in the composition and \(M_i\) is its atomic mass. Consequently, the descriptor represents the average atomic mass weighted by the elemental stoichiometry rather than an unweighted average of the constituent atomic masses. It provides a compact numerical representation of compositional differences that can be used alongside the individual site-fraction and ion-count descriptors.

The feature \texttt{Perovskite\_avg\_radius} represents an average ionic-radius descriptor for the perovskite composition. It summarizes the ionic sizes associated with the constituent species and complements the site-specific composition descriptors by providing a scalar representation of the composition's characteristic ionic size. The resulting values depend on the ionic-radius reference data and the weighting procedure used during feature construction.

The feature \texttt{perovskite\_octahedral\_factor} represents a geometric descriptor of the perovskite composition. For an idealized \(ABX_3\) composition, the octahedral factor is defined as

\[
\mu=\frac{r_B}{r_X},
\]

where \(r_B\) and \(r_X\) are the ionic radii of the B-site cation and X-site anion, respectively. This ratio characterizes the relative sizes of the ions forming the \(BX_6\) octahedral framework. For mixed compositions, its calculation depends on how the effective B-site and X-site ionic radii are obtained from the constituent species. The octahedral factor was included as a composition-derived predictor; its inclusion does not, by itself, establish structural stability or a causal relationship with power conversion efficiency.

\subsubsection{Material-Property Augmentation}
\label{sec:matprop}

A separate material-property reference dataset was curated for standardized material identifiers with an occurrence frequency of at least 100 across the parsed ETL, HTL, and perovskite stacks. Available electronic and physicochemical properties were compiled from published literature and, where applicable, retrieved through the Materials Project database \cite{jain2013materialsproject}. These values represent general material-level reference properties rather than measurements reported for the specific PSC devices in which the materials appeared. Property availability was heterogeneous, and not every material had a complete set of descriptors.

The curated property catalogue included band gap, highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy, lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy, electron mobility, hole mobility, dielectric constant, and material classification, together with the material identifier and occurrence frequency. Source references were associated with the curated values to support traceability.

For each device, property lookup was performed separately for the ETL, HTL, and perovskite layers. Within each layer family, the recorded material entries were traversed in their original order, and the first available non-missing value was assigned for each property. This procedure produced descriptors such as \texttt{ETL\_band\_gap}, \texttt{HTL\_HOMO}, and \texttt{Perovskite\_dielectric\_constant}. Because the lookup was performed independently for each property, the material supplying one descriptor was not necessarily the same material supplying another descriptor for a given layer family. Missing property values were retained during this stage and handled during model feature preparation.

\subsubsection{PDP-Derived Process and Device Features}

The composition descriptors were combined with relevant device and fabrication variables retained from the original PDP export. These variables included available layer-thickness information, deposition methods and solvents, annealing conditions, and device-related flags. Together with the composition descriptors they form the PDP-derived tier $t_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:tiers}). Because the parsed material identifiers were not used as predictors, $t_1$ contains no explicit information on which transport-layer materials were used; that information enters the model only through the material-property descriptors of $t_2$.

\subsubsection{LLM-Derived Recipe Flags ($t_3$)}
\label{sec:recipe}
The LLM-derived part of $t_3$ was built from the stored extraction JSON (no new LLM calls and no further full-text retrieval) by deterministic matching of canonicalised material names and process fields. It consists of 36 binary paper-level recipe flags (\texttt{rc\_*}), intended to encode recipe information that the PDP columns do not hold as explicit identities: 14 additive and precursor flags (for example chloride and thiocyanate sources, acetate, SnF$_2$, 5-AVA, hydrohalic acids, alkali and Cs additives, PEA-family spacers, Lewis bases, polymer, water and DIO additives, carbon-based contacts); 6 hole-transport-layer dopant flags (TBP, LiTFSI, FK-type dopants, F4-TCNQ, Cu salts, borates); 2 electron-transport-layer flags (TiCl$_4$ treatment, other dopants); undoped-layer and interface-passivation flags (2); 7 process-route flags (antisolvent, vacuum, two-step, hot casting, solvent annealing, nitrogen box, ambient); and 5 device-context flags (flexible, tandem, lead-free, inorganic, mesoscopic). A flag is 1 if the pattern is found in the extracted records of the publication and 0 otherwise, so it means ``mentioned in the extraction'', not necessarily ``used in every device of the paper''. The flags are missing for publications without an extraction record, which covers 15,027 of 41,107 records (36.6\%; 2,731 publications), and are passed to CatBoost as native missing values without missingness indicators. $t_3$ is $t_2$ plus these 36 flags; in the models of Section~\ref{sec:three} this gives 215 predictor columns (179 of $t_2$ plus 36), verified on the fitted models. A CatBoost first-feature-use penalty of 0.5 is applied to the flag columns. Photovoltaic outcome fields were not used. The flags are paper-level outputs of pattern matching over LLM-extracted JSON, so they are noisy, were not validated against annotation, and cannot distinguish devices within a publication; one flag (hot casting) is positive for a single publication and is effectively constant. The earlier 44-predictor literature block (previous subsection) is a different representation and is not a subset of $t_3$.

\subsection{Data Integration, Filtering and Feature Tiers}
\label{sec:integration}

The structured device-level feature table and the DOI-level literature feature table were integrated using the publication DOI as the join key. DOI strings were normalised before matching and before grouping by removing byte-order marks and whitespace, lower-casing, and stripping \texttt{doi.org} and \texttt{doi:} prefixes; this merges 45 case/prefix variants, so the 7,371 raw DOI strings of the filtered data correspond to 7,326 publications. A left join was performed from the device-level table to the DOI-level feature table, retaining the device records even when a corresponding literature-feature record was unavailable. Missing paper-level features arising from unsuccessful retrieval, incomplete extraction, or unmatched DOI identifiers were preserved as missing values for subsequent model preparation.

The initial PDP export contained 43,398 records associated with 7,499 unique raw DOI strings. We excluded 1,002 records with missing target values and a further 1,289 records with reported PCE below 1.0\%, giving 41,107 records from 7,326 publications plus 232 records without a DOI (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). Of these, 28,340 records were n-i-p and 12,684 were p-i-n devices; the remaining 83 records have other or unspecified architectures and were used only in the pooled model. The filtered set contains 17 records (six publications) with PCE above 27\%, i.e.\ above the certified single-junction record; the enriched table carries no illumination or tandem field with which to remove them on physical grounds, so they were retained under the pre-declared filter. Excluding them from the test sets changes the pooled $t_2$ $R^2$ by $+0.003$ (0.408 to 0.410), so they do not drive any conclusion. The DOI was used exclusively as a record-linkage and grouping variable and was never a predictor. The 232 records without a DOI were treated as one explicit group that is never divided between partitions; this is conservative, because their true publications are unknown. Free-text columns were removed. \subsection{Machine Learning Models and Evaluation}

\subsubsection{Prediction task and feature tiers.}
\label{sec:tiers}
We formulated PCE prediction as a supervised regression task, with experimentally reported PCE as the target. Three nested feature tiers are used throughout (Table~\ref{tab:tiertable}): $t_1$, the PDP-derived device, processing, architecture and composition features; $t_2$, $t_1$ plus curated material-property descriptors; and $t_3$, $t_2$ plus the 36 LLM-derived recipe flags (Section~\ref{sec:recipe}). Nesting is enforced in code and asserted in the test suite. The three tiers are compared on the same reserved publications (Section~\ref{sec:v4protocol}). Earlier experiments with repeated DOI-grouped holdouts, record-level and stricter protocols, and the reference and tuned models used $t_1$ and $t_2$ only; those experiments also evaluated an earlier 44-predictor literature block, which is not $t_3$, is not part of the tiers and is reported in the Supplementary Information. Each tier was evaluated on the pooled dataset and, in the five-holdout experiments, separately on the p-i-n and n-i-p subsets, which were modelled independently. There, $t_2$ plus 32 columns of N(0,1) noise served as a negative control (Supplementary Information).

\subsubsection{Model training.}
\label{sec:models}
Models were trained with CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}, using its native handling of the categorical variables (architecture and the deposition methods). To ensure a controlled comparison across feature tiers, all tiers used a single CatBoost regressor with the same fixed hyperparameters (Table~\ref{tab:config}). These values were fixed a priori and inherited from an earlier configuration of this project that was not tuned in the present study; the number of trees was chosen by early stopping on a DOI-disjoint validation set and no tier was tuned. The only difference between tiers is the predictor set, so the tier comparisons are feature ablations rather than comparisons between model architectures, hyperparameters or ensembling. Separately tuned configurations are used only where stated (Section~\ref{sec:recipe_results}) and are never mixed with the matched comparison. Reference models were trained on the same splits and features: a training-mean predictor; ridge regression (one-hot categorical encoding, standardised and winsorised numerical features, penalty chosen on the validation set); XGBoost with library defaults (the setting of Roberts-style pipelines); a random forest (300 trees); a multilayer perceptron (two hidden layers, early stopping on the validation set); and an exact Gaussian process on a fixed random subsample of 3,000 training records (exact GPR scales cubically with the number of records). A separate model-development experiment, \emph{not} used for the tier comparison, selected CatBoost depth and L2 regularisation from a six-point grid by validation RMSE (identical grid for every tier), then averaged three seeds, and combined this bag with an early-stopped XGBoost model.

\subsubsection{Evaluation protocol with a DOI-disjoint validation set.}
To evaluate generalization to previously unseen publications, we used five repeated DOI-grouped holdout splits generated with \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} (test fraction 0.2 of publications, seed 42), so that records sharing a normalised DOI were always assigned to the same partition (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). Within each training partition, a further DOI-disjoint validation set (15\% of the training publications) was used for early stopping and, where applicable, model selection; the test partition was used only for the final evaluation, and the model was not refitted on the validation records. No DOI occurs in more than one of the training, validation and test partitions in any split; this is recorded in every run record and checked by automated tests. Because repeated group-shuffle splits may have overlapping test sets, the evaluation is a repeated DOI-grouped holdout evaluation rather than mutually exclusive five-fold cross-validation, and the standard deviations reported across splits describe split-to-split variability rather than confidence intervals. The record-level comparison protocol used \texttt{ShuffleSplit} with the same fractions, seed scheme, tiers, preprocessing and models; its validation set is a random subset of the training records. Record-level fits keep improving on a random validation set for much longer than DOI-grouped fits (a first run with the 1,500-iteration cap truncated 31 of 45 record-level CatBoost fits). The record-level arm was therefore rerun with a cap of 6,000 iterations for all subsets, and for the pooled data again with a cap of 20,000 iterations, where all 15 fits stopped on their own (best iteration 7,207--11,702); the pooled record-level numbers reported here are the converged ones. DOI-grouped fits stop before 500 iterations and are unaffected by the cap. Two stricter protocols were run for the pooled and architecture-specific models with the same tiers, preprocessing, models and validation scheme. \emph{Lead-author grouping} assigns each publication the modal PDP lead-author surname (a proxy for laboratory; common surnames such as Wang, Zhang and Li make some groups large and heterogeneous, so this over-groups) and splits by that key with the same \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} settings; records without DOI remain one block. \emph{Forward-in-time splitting} dates each publication by its earliest PDP record and, for three non-overlapping test windows covering publication-date quantiles (0.55, 0.70], (0.70, 0.85] and (0.85, 1.00] (test dates 2018-05 to 2024-08), trains on strictly earlier publications, with the most recent 15\% of those as validation set; all partitions are DOI-disjoint by construction. Paired differences between tiers were assessed within each split by a cluster bootstrap that resamples test publications with replacement (1,000 resamples) and reports the per-split 95\% percentile interval; because the test sets of different repeats overlap, no pooled significance test is reported.

\subsubsection{Common-split evaluation of the three tiers.}
\label{sec:v4protocol}
$t_1$, $t_2$ and $t_3$ were compared under one protocol fixed after the exploratory ablation. Publications were assigned by a salted DOI hash to a development partition (65\%; 26,982 records, 4,708 publications) used for all design, tuning and ablation work, and a reserved partition (35\%; 14,125 records, 2,619 publications, of which 5,460 records from 1,005 publications have extraction data). The reserved publications had appeared in earlier experiments with other feature representations, so they are new for the recipe-flag representation but not untouched data, and the flag block was selected after comparisons on development publications. For the main comparison all three tiers used the matched default CatBoost configuration (Table~\ref{tab:config}), the same development records for training, the same DOI-disjoint 15\% validation set per seed for early stopping, training-only preprocessing, and the same five seeds; the final prediction is the mean of the five seed models, and all tiers are scored on identical reserved records. Models were trained once on all development records. The $t_2$ and $t_3$ arms were scored first; the $t_1$ arm was added afterwards in the same script path, with the same seeds and validation splits, and no choice depended on its result. Scores are reported for the covered subset (reserved records from publications with extraction data) and for all reserved records, where records of publications without extraction data carry missing flags under the missing-feature policy above. Paired differences between tiers were assessed by a publication-cluster bootstrap of the squared error (2,000 resamples), and per-tier $R^2$ intervals by the same bootstrap. To examine configuration dependence of the $t_2 \rightarrow t_3$ step only, an equal-budget random search of 24 CatBoost configurations (the matched default included) was run for $t_2$ and $t_3$ separately and scored by DOI-grouped five-fold mean squared error on development publications; $t_1$ was not tuned. Two comparisons then isolate different questions: \emph{same settings} gives both tiers the same configuration (the default, or one tier's tuned configuration), so that only the added features differ; \emph{each tuned} gives each tier its own selected configuration, which mixes feature and tuning effects. Negative controls replaced the flag block by five DOI-permuted copies or by a coverage indicator alone. Because this partition and protocol differ from the five repeated holdouts below, absolute $R^2$ values from the two protocols are not comparable.

\subsubsection{Performance metrics.}
Predictive performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean squared error (RMSE), calculated by comparing experimentally reported PCE values, $y_i$, with predictions, $\hat{y}_i$, for the $N$ records in each held-out test partition:
\begin{equation}
    R^2 =
    1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}
    {\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\bar{y})^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE} =
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_i-\hat{y}_i|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE} =
    \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{y}$ is the mean experimentally reported PCE in the corresponding test partition. MAE and RMSE are expressed in PCE percentage points. The coefficient of determination measures performance relative to a constant-mean reference, whereas MAE and RMSE quantify absolute prediction errors, with RMSE assigning greater weight to large deviations.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Model Interpretation}

SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) was used to interpret fitted CatBoost models \cite{shappaper}. The SHAP analysis was computed for matched CatBoost models trained on $t_2$ plus the earlier 44-predictor literature block, not for $t_1$, $t_2$ or $t_3$ models. It used \texttt{TreeExplainer} on a random sample of 1,500 held-out records from the first DOI-grouped split, with mean absolute SHAP values as global importance, for the pooled, p-i-n and n-i-p models; features were aggregated into feature groups. Because the p-i-n and n-i-p models were trained on different records, differences between their rankings describe what each model learned rather than different physical effects in the two architectures.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Three feature tiers on reserved publications}
\label{sec:three}

Table~\ref{tab:three} and Fig.~\ref{fig:three} compare $t_1$, $t_2$ and $t_3$ on the same reserved publications (Section~\ref{sec:v4protocol}): one fixed CatBoost configuration, identical training, validation and test publications, DOI-disjoint partitions, and identical test records within each population. On the 5,460 reserved records from 1,005 publications with LLM-derived extraction data, $R^2$ increased from 0.318 ($t_1$) to 0.359 ($t_2$) and 0.366 ($t_3$), with MAE falling from 3.021 to 2.911 and 2.892 and RMSE from 3.866 to 3.747 and 3.726 percentage points. On all 14,125 reserved records from 2,619 publications the corresponding values were 0.371, 0.403 and 0.406 ($R^2$), 3.054, 2.955 and 2.949 (MAE) and 3.896, 3.794 and 3.787 (RMSE). The sequence is therefore monotonic in all three metrics in both populations. The two steps differ in size and in certainty (Table~\ref{tab:paired3}). The material-property step $t_1 \rightarrow t_2$ is clear: $\Delta R^2 = +0.042$ (95\% interval $+0.021$ to $+0.063$) on covered records and $+0.032$ ($+0.021$ to $+0.044$) on all records, positive in all five seed models. The recipe-flag step $t_2 \rightarrow t_3$ is small: $\Delta R^2 = +0.0071$ ($-0.0007$ to $+0.0157$; positive in 4 of 5 seeds) on covered records and $+0.0025$ ($-0.0011$ to $+0.0062$; 4 of 5 seeds) on all records, with intervals that include zero. We therefore describe the trend $t_1 < t_2 < t_3$ as directionally consistent, with a statistically established first step and a small, not statistically established second step; the second step is larger where the flags exist (covered records) than on all records, where 61\% of the reserved records have no flags. The per-tier intervals in Table~\ref{tab:three} overlap widely because they are dominated by between-publication variability shared by all tiers; the paired intervals are the relevant uncertainty for differences. $R^2$ on the covered subset is lower than on all records for every tier, which reflects a different population and PCE variance and not a property of the flags.

\subsection{Recipe flags: configuration dependence and controls}
\label{sec:recipe_results}
The recipe-flag block was developed on development publications only. There, the remaining content of the extraction JSON (token bags, numeric quantities, device-matched layer values, gap-filling of PDP fields) added at most about $+0.004$ $R^2$ to $t_2$, and giving $t_2$ the PDP's own curated process columns for the same papers added only $+0.022$, which motivated a block of recipe identities. On covered development publications the flags gave $+0.007$ $R^2$ (12 of 15 folds), five DOI-permuted copies gave $-0.009$ to $+0.002$, dropping any one flag group reduced the gain, and no single group reproduced it, so the signal is distributed and close to the noise floor. A role-scoped variant of the flags gave $+0.001$, so differences between variants are within development noise.

The reserved-publication results of Table~\ref{tab:three} used the same default configuration for every tier. To separate the effect of the added features from that of hyperparameter selection, Table~\ref{tab:v4} gives the $t_2 \rightarrow t_3$ step under other settings. When each tier used its own tuned configuration, covered-record $R^2$ was 0.361 for $t_2$ and 0.371 for $t_3$ ($\Delta R^2 = +0.0094$, $-0.0016$ to $+0.0219$; 5 of 5 seeds), and on all records both tiers reached about 0.41 ($\Delta R^2 = +0.0015$, $-0.0039$ to $+0.0067$; 3 of 5 seeds). These tuned comparisons are not part of the three-tier sequence: $t_1$ was not tuned, and the tuned arms mix feature and tuning effects. In point estimate the flags exceed all five permuted-block controls under default settings (covered $-0.0049$ to $+0.0015$; all records $-0.0028$ to $+0.0007$) and a coverage-indicator-only control ($+0.0015$ and $+0.0011$). Every interval, however, includes zero. We read these results as a small, suggestive improvement that is concentrated where the information exists, not as an established gain in generalisation.

Four features of the evidence limit how far it can be pushed. First, the size of the effect depends on the estimator: with $t_2$'s tuned configuration used for both tiers the covered $\Delta R^2$ falls to $+0.0024$ ($-0.0077$ to $+0.0131$) and the all-record difference is $-0.0016$. Second, tuning alone moves $t_2$ by $+0.0020$ (covered) to $+0.0030$ (all records), comparable to the flag effect, so the tuned comparison should not be read as a feature gain by itself. Third, the flag block was chosen after development comparisons, and the reserved publications, though not used for any choice in this representation, were examined in earlier experiments with other feature sets. Fourth, a single reserved split of about 1,000 covered publications resolves $R^2$ differences only to roughly $\pm 0.01$. The flags are also available for only 36.6\% of records, which dilutes any benefit on the full data; a larger signal would require full-text extraction for the roughly 4,500 publications that currently lack it, which we did not do. A plausible reason for the small size of the effect is redundancy: the extracted numerical parameters largely duplicate PDP columns already in $t_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), and recipe identities may partly be inferable from the device and material descriptors, so what remains is a small residual; we did not test this explanation. The results in Table~\ref{tab:v4} use the reserved partition of Section~\ref{sec:three}; they must not be combined with the five-holdout tables below.

\subsection{Effect of the evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:splits}

The evaluation protocol had a large influence on measured performance. Table~\ref{tab:split} and Fig.~\ref{fig:split} compare record-level random splitting with DOI-grouped splitting across the feature tiers with identical features, preprocessing, model configurations, filtering and training budgets; only the unit that is split differs. For matched CatBoost the pooled $R^2$ was 0.587 and 0.618 under record-level splitting for $t_1$ and $t_2$, compared with 0.368 and 0.408 under DOI-grouped splitting, a difference of 0.219 and 0.210. MAE rose from 2.23--2.34 to 2.95--3.08 percentage points. The gap is therefore similar for both tiers. The record-level fits needed 7,200--11,700 iterations (DOI-grouped fits stop before 500), and the gap was 0.005--0.007 smaller when the cap was 6,000 iterations, so it is sensitive to training length but not to the cap once converged.

Two properties of the data make a large gap plausible. First, 69\% of the variance of PCE lies between publications (one-way intraclass correlation by DOI, 0.69), with a median of four records per publication. Second, for the earlier representation of $t_2$ plus the 44-predictor literature block (the only one for which this audit was run), 31,591 of 41,107 records (77\%) share their complete predictor vector with at least one other record, but only 162 of 7,733 such groups span more than one publication, so near-duplicate devices are almost entirely within-publication; their PCE values differ by a median standard deviation of 1.2 points within a group. A flexible model can memorise publication-specific patterns that a random split leaves available at test time. This reading is consistent with the linear model: for the pooled data ridge regression shows a much smaller gap (0.055--0.056), but it is not a clean capacity test, because the ridge gap for the p-i-n subset is large (0.11--0.18) and ridge is also a weaker predictor on all splits; we did not run a matched-capacity control. The comparison quantifies sensitivity of the measured performance to the splitting protocol on this dataset; it does not show that all of the difference is leakage of identical devices, because the two protocols also produce test sets with different publication compositions, and the stricter lead-author and forward-in-time protocols of Section~\ref{sec:strict} show how much of the DOI-grouped score survives (a little under lead-author grouping, about 0.06 $R^2$ less going forward in time); it still does not amount to an external test. DOI-grouped evaluation answers a specific question, namely performance on papers not seen in training. It does not remove similarity between papers from the same laboratory, and it is not a prospective or external validation. Earlier PDP studies report a larger drop on newly registered records (Fukasawa et al.: $R^2$ 0.7177 under an 8:2 split to 0.45 on 294 newer records) \cite{fukasawa2024effectiveness}, which is directionally consistent, although the targets, feature sets, dataset versions and test sets differ and the numbers are not comparable.

\subsection{Stricter validation: laboratory proxy and forward-in-time splits}
\label{sec:strict}

DOI-grouped splitting leaves two obvious routes for residual similarity: papers from the same laboratory and papers close in time. Table~\ref{tab:strict} compares both with DOI-grouped and record-level evaluation (pooled data, all models and tiers matched). The temporal result is based on three windows of one dataset snapshot and a partly overlapping training history, so its spread should not be over-interpreted; it indicates that the DOI-grouped estimate of about 0.41 is optimistic for prospective use by roughly 0.06 in $R^2$. These protocols were not run for $t_3$.

\subsection{Contribution of material-property descriptors}
\label{sec:features}

Adding material-property descriptors ($t_1 \rightarrow t_2$) increased $R^2$ by 0.040 (pooled), 0.047 (p-i-n) and 0.029 (n-i-p) in the five-holdout experiment and lowered RMSE by 0.125, 0.130 and 0.093 percentage points (Table~\ref{tab:delta}), consistent with the larger gain on the reserved publications (Table~\ref{tab:paired3}). The pooled gain had a cluster-bootstrap interval excluding zero in all five splits (two of five for p-i-n and three of five for n-i-p, which have fewer test publications), and $R^2$ improved in all 15 split--subset pairs. Because $t_1$ contains no explicit transport-layer material identities (Section~\ref{sec:integration}), the $t_2$ descriptors convey both which materials were used and their reference properties; each property is a fixed function of the material, so this gain could reflect knowledge of which materials were used rather than their property values. A material-identity control separates the two. Adding only the ETL, HTL and perovskite identities to $t_1$ as native CatBoost categorical features (disjoint DOI-grouped five-fold, three seeds, same CatBoost configuration, 41,107 records) gives $R^2 = 0.406$, against 0.370 for $t_1$ and 0.407 for $t_2$; the 102 descriptor columns add $+0.001$ over identity alone (interval for the paired MSE difference includes zero), and descriptors on top of identity add $+0.004$ (also not distinguishable from zero). The gain of $t_2$ over $t_1$ is therefore attributable to knowing which transport and absorber materials a device contains, not to the physical descriptor values; transferable physical information is not demonstrated by this comparison (a leave-material-out test would be needed). This is consistent with prior work showing that the representation of material and process information strongly affects PCE prediction \cite{fukasawa2024effectiveness}, and it shows that the benefit persists when entire publications are held out.

The same five-holdout protocol was also used for an earlier, superseded LLM-derived representation, a block of 44 literature predictors. Added to $t_2$ it produced no measurable change ($\Delta R^2 = -0.001$ pooled; no interval excluded zero in any of 15 split--subset pairs), and controls (the block without material properties, without two reporting-completeness flags, and $t_2$ plus 32 noise columns) pointed the same way; the details are in the Supplementary Information. That null result, together with the redundancy of the extracted numerical parameters with PDP columns (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), motivated the more targeted recipe-flag representation $t_3$. It does not bear on the size of the recipe-flag effect, which is evaluated only in Sections~\ref{sec:three} and~\ref{sec:recipe_results}.

\subsection{Interpretation of model predictions}
\label{sec:shap}

SHAP values are attributions for the fitted model and do not demonstrate predictive benefit or causal effects. The SHAP analysis was computed for models trained on $t_2$ plus the earlier 44-predictor literature block, not for the recipe-flag model $t_3$; its figures and rankings are therefore given in the Supplementary Information (Figs.~S1 and~S2) and are not evidence about $t_3$. In those models perovskite quenching/crystallisation (the PDP binary field \texttt{Perovskite\_deposition\_quenching\_induced\_crystallisation}) was the highest-ranked predictor in the pooled, p-i-n and n-i-p models, and aggregating importance by feature group showed that material-property (33--38\%) and process (36--42\%) variables dominate, followed by composition (13--15\%) and device (6--8\%) descriptors; the earlier literature block received 3.6--4.0\%. Because material properties are fixed functions of material identity, layer-property features such as HTL band gap also act as identifiers of the material used in that layer, and the rankings of the p-i-n and n-i-p models describe what each model learned from different records, not different physical effects. We did not compute SHAP attributions for the 36 recipe flags.

\subsection{Limitations}
Results come from a single database with heterogeneous reporting, and 69\% of the PCE variance lies between publications, so held-out $R^2$ values near 0.4 mean that most between-paper variation is not explained by the available descriptors. The DOI-grouped holdout measures generalization to unseen publications, but the five repeated test sets overlap, so the reported spreads are not those of an independent repeated grouped cross-validation, and the cluster-bootstrap intervals address within-test dependence only. Grouping by DOI does not separate publications from the same research group, which may share materials and recipes across papers; grouping by lead-author surname is only a coarse laboratory proxy, and the forward-in-time evaluation (three windows, Section~\ref{sec:strict}) lowers $R^2$ to about 0.35; neither is an external validation on independently collected devices. The 232 records without a DOI were kept together, which is conservative but not informative about their true publications. Some publications report the same device configuration (1,277 records in 162 groups of identical predictor vectors of the earlier $t_2$-plus-literature-block representation span more than one DOI), and such cross-publication near-duplicates can still fall on both sides of a DOI-grouped split. LLM-derived features were available for about 37\% of the records (36.6\% for the recipe flags; 36.2\% for the earlier block, from a coverage mask that differs by 32 publications whose cause we did not investigate), extraction accuracy was assessed only against a second curated source for an earlier feature table and not against a hand-annotated gold set, the recipe flags themselves were not validated, and the flags are defined per publication and therefore cannot distinguish between devices within the same paper. The recipe-flag evidence rests on one reserved partition of about 1,000 covered publications (resolution roughly $\pm 0.01$ $R^2$), the flag block was chosen after development comparisons, the reserved publications had been examined in earlier experiments with other feature representations, and the apparent gain depends on the CatBoost configuration; all intervals for the $t_2 \rightarrow t_3$ step include zero. The three-tier comparison is a single partition; the five-holdout ablation, the model-development table, the SHAP analysis, the reference models and the stricter protocols do not include $t_3$, and no five-split ablation of the recipe flags was run. Material-property descriptors are material-level reference values, so they identify materials as much as they describe them. The reference Gaussian process was fitted to a subsample and the MLP was not extensively tuned, so Table~\ref{tab:baselines} does not rank model families. The retrieval route of the historical full-text corpus is not asserted to be exclusively legal (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), and the extraction model checkpoint is not recorded. Predictions saturate near 21\% PCE, under-predict high-efficiency devices, can be negative, and devices with reported PCE below 1\% were excluded.

\section{Conclusion}
We built and audited a PCE prediction pipeline for the Perovskite Database Project and evaluated it on unseen publications with DOI-grouped, validation-based holdouts. Two findings are supported by the evidence. First, performance on unseen publications is moderate, and it rises with the feature tier in a way that is clear for one step and small for the other. On identical reserved publications and test records with one fixed CatBoost configuration, $R^2$ rose from 0.371 ($t_1$, PDP-derived features) to 0.403 ($t_2$, plus curated material properties) and 0.406 ($t_3$, plus 36 LLM-derived recipe flags) on all 14,125 reserved records, and from 0.318 to 0.359 and 0.366 on the 5,460 records whose publications have extraction data (MAE 3.02, 2.91 and 2.89 percentage points). The sequence is monotonic in $R^2$, MAE and RMSE in both populations. The material-property step is well supported ($\Delta R^2 = +0.042$ covered, +0.032 all records, intervals excluding zero), and in a five-holdout experiment it is reproduced by adding only the material identities to $t_1$ ($R^2$ 0.406 versus 0.407), so it reflects knowing which materials are present. The recipe-flag step is small ($+0.0071$ covered, $+0.0025$ all; $+0.0094$ and $+0.0015$ with separately tuned settings), all four 95\% intervals include zero, the effect depends on the CatBoost configuration, the flags were selected on development comparisons, and the reserved publications had appeared in earlier experiments with other feature sets; we therefore regard it as a small, suggestive signal that the additive and recipe identities carry some information the database columns lack, not as an established improvement. Larger coverage from full-text extraction would be needed to test it, and the five-holdout protocol, reference models and stricter validations were not run for $t_3$. A validation-tuned CatBoost--XGBoost ensemble on $t_2$ reached a pooled $R^2$ of 0.416 (MAE 2.92) in the five-holdout experiment, with substantial architecture dependence (n-i-p 0.41, p-i-n 0.30) and a split-to-split standard deviation of about 0.03; simple ridge regression and a properly trained MLP or Gaussian process are 0.03--0.07 $R^2$ below matched CatBoost at $t_1$. Second, evaluation by record rather than by publication inflates the measured $R^2$ for a flexible model by 0.21--0.22 on this data (CatBoost: 0.59--0.62 versus 0.37--0.41), whereas a low-capacity ridge model shows a gap of 0.06; because 69\% of PCE variance lies between publications and most near-duplicate devices are within a publication, we recommend reporting DOI-grouped (or stricter) estimates alongside record-level ones for literature-compiled PSC data. Under forward-in-time evaluation $R^2$ falls to 0.345 (MAE 3.18), and lead-author grouping gives 0.394; the results do not establish performance on independently collected external data, for tandem or non-standard devices, or for high-efficiency devices, which are systematically under-predicted.
