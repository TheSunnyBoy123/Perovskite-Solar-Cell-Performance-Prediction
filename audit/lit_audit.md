## 2. Roberts et al. (2024) and Fukasawa et al. (2024)

### Roberts et al. 2024 (Adv. Phys. Res. 3, 2400060)
Verified from search-returned abstract text (Wiley, via search tool): data = Perovskite Database Project, "over 43 000 entries from published literature"; target = short-circuit current density Jsc; three ML architectures; "using the XGBoost architecture, a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 3.58, R2 of 0.35 and a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 9.49% are achieved"; an open-source data-cleaning pipeline is released on GitHub.
Verified from the precursor master's thesis (N. Roberts, "FAIR Modeling for Perovskite Solar Cells: An Open Source Machine Learning Pipeline", Univ. of Washington, 2023; PDF read): "For this study, an 80/10/10 split was used" (train/validation/test; no grouping by DOI mentioned); after cleaning 18,320 entries and removing duplicates 7,154 remain; duplicate Jsc values were replaced by "the average of the mean and median of the distribution"; "The average number of DOIs per distribution is 1.04", i.e. the duplicate groups come almost entirely from a single paper; the thesis reports XGBoost RMSE 3.73 and R = 0.63 (R-value, different from the journal R2). Thesis numbers differ from the journal abstract (RMSE 3.58, R2 0.35).
UNVERIFIED: whether the journal paper itself uses a random split (the thesis does; the journal protocol was not readable), the journal's record counts and feature counts.
Claims in ml.tex vs. evidence:
- "evaluated XGBoost, ANN, and GPR for predicting Jsc; XGBoost test R2 0.35, RMSE 3.58 mA cm^-2": ACCURATE (abstract). Units mA cm^-2 consistent with Jsc.
- "documented substantial duplication of device configurations in the PDP and addressed it by averaging target values within duplicate groups" (line 89): supported by the thesis, but note nuance: the thesis found only 154 exact duplicates in the raw database, and the ~60% reduction (18,320 -> 7,154) was CREATED by their feature selection/encoding. So "duplication in the PDP" is mildly misleading; say "duplication of encoded device configurations after feature reduction". Also, that 1.04 DOIs per duplicate group means the duplicates are within-paper, which supports (but does not itself show) the within-publication-dependence argument; this is a useful positive citation for the paper's motivation. Journal-version confirmation needed (UNVERIFIED).
- Do not state Roberts used random splits unless you read the journal paper; you can say "the thesis version of the pipeline used a random 80/10/10 split".

### Fukasawa, Asahi, Taniguchi 2024 (Energy Adv. 3, 812-820)
Verified from search-tool text drawn from the paper (RSC/ScienceDirect snippets): "The dataset was downloaded from the Perovskite Database, which contained 42 459 records as of 31 March 2022."; "The dataset was split into 8 : 2 for training and test, respectively."; models tested: random forest, neural network, gradient-boosted decision tree; abstract: "the split of process information influenced the prediction accuracy"; "limitation of performance prediction was also prone to data degeneracy"; "interpretation of the machine learning model allowed us to find the relative influence of materials and processes on the PCE". So the primary split is a random 80:20 split of records, with no stated publication grouping.
UNVERIFIED (could not retrieve full text or SI): 36,937 curated records; 248 variables; R2 = 0.7177 on the random split; "294 newly registered records" with R2 = 0.45 and MAE = 3.20%; 6,446 degenerate points covering 20,695 feature vectors. I found no source that contradicts them and the paper title/abstract ("limitation... data degeneracy") is consistent with them, but the numbers are NOT confirmed by me. Before submission someone must open https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/ya/d3ya00617d in a browser and check: (a) 294 records and the registration date window, (b) R2 0.45 and MAE 3.20 (is the unit % or percentage points; is the MAE in PCE %), (c) whether the 294 records belong to publications absent from the training set, (d) whether the 0.7177 is for the same model/feature set as the 0.45.
Claim risk in ml.tex: "Fukasawa et al. reported a reduction ... from R2=0.7177 under conventional evaluation to R2=0.45" (line 89). A cautious reading is a temporal-style hold-out on new records; whether it is also publication-disjoint is unknown to me. ## 4. Novelty: recommended wording and overreach

Recommended narrow novelty statement (2-4 sentences):
"Prior PDP-based PCE and Jsc models have generally been assessed on record-level random splits or small sets of newly registered records, and we are not aware of a published study that compares record-level and DOI-grouped evaluation on the same PDP export under matched features and model settings. Using repeated DOI-grouped holdout splits, we find that the pooled R2 falls from 0.55-0.66 to 0.37-0.41 across three nested feature tiers. We also test whether publication-level features extracted from full texts with a locally hosted LLM add information beyond database descriptors and curated material properties; the gain is small (Delta R2 = 0.005 to 0.007) and we do not claim it is statistically established."
Hedge "we are not aware" must be kept; the search was not exhaustive, and Fukasawa's new-record test and Roberts' dedup work already make related points.

Claims in ml.tex that overreach or are unsafe:
1. Abstract: "they are typically evaluated with record-level random splits". Supported only for Fukasawa (8:2 split, search snippet), Roberts thesis (80/10/10) and unstated for Hussain. Soften to "often" and cite them.
2. Abstract/conclusion: "publication-level evaluation is required for realistic performance estimates". A recommendation, not a result of this study; say "should be reported alongside" and note a DOI-grouped split still leaks lab/author similarity (your own Limitations says so).
3. Abstract: "Record-level random splitting overestimates R2 by 0.17-0.24". The random-vs-grouped gap is the metric difference between two protocols, not a proven bias of one; also the 0.24 for t3 vs 0.17 for t1/t2 ("largest inflation for the literature-augmented model") is a single-pass comparison without uncertainty; fine to report, but a causal reading (literature features leak paper identity) must be hedged or tested. Per-publication features are constant within a paper, so under a random split they act as paper identifiers: this is a real mechanism and worth stating, but it undercuts the claim that LLM features are "useful" in the random-split setting.
4. LLM-feature claim: "small but consistent improvement in every architecture subset and every error metric". The paired SDs (0.006-0.007) are as large as the mean gains, test sets overlap, no significance test, and one author-todo asks how many of 5 splits improved. SHAP shows the top literature feature is json_numeric_values_found (reporting completeness), and only 36.2% of records have LLM features. So the safer statement is "no detectable degradation and a small, within-noise improvement", not "consistent improvement".
5. Intro: "Building on earlier evidence that performance can decline on newly registered records" depends on the unverified Fukasawa numbers (294, 0.45, 3.20).
6. Line 89: "Roberts ... documented substantial duplication of device configurations in the PDP": see Section 2, duplication arose from their encoding (raw PDP: 154 duplicates), not PDP itself.
7. Comparison table (line 455+): Roberts-based and Fukasawa-based pipelines are reimplementations with different targets/feature engineering; call them "re-implementations" and state which hyperparameters were used; Fukasawa row is still empty. The 0.403 vs 0.368 difference is on one split, no uncertainty; do not claim superiority.
8. "Overall R2 of 0.41 +/- 0.01": SD over 5 overlapping holdouts understates uncertainty; label as split-to-split SD only (the Limitations paragraph already does this; move the caveat into the abstract or table note).
9. Housekeeping inconsistencies: abstract says 2,726 articles; Section 2 says 3,299 PDFs retrieved and 3,141 Markdown documents; reconcile which count is used for LLM features. Line 310 has a typo "0.403e". The abstract carries an \authortodo that says all numbers will change after re-running with a DOI-disjoint early-stopping set; numbers in this audit's recommended wording must be updated then. Section 2.3.3 vs 2.5.3 cross-refs not checked.
10. Title "Leakage-Aware DOI Evaluation": the study's own Limitations say DOI grouping does not separate groups/labs; "leakage-aware" is acceptable, "leakage-free" would not be.

## 5. Digital Discovery (RSC) policies

Direct fetches of rsc.org pages returned HTTP 403, so the items below come from search-tool excerpts of the official pages; re-check in a browser before submission.
- Author guidelines: https://www.rsc.org/publishing/publish-with-us/publish-a-journal-article/digital-discovery (excerpt): authors are expected to submit code and data to community-recognised repositories, or a general repository if none exists; custom code referenced in the manuscript must be deposited in a persistent repository (e.g. Zenodo, Code Ocean, Mendeley) upon acceptance; Zenodo is recommended for GitHub-hosted code; DOIs for the latest and archived versions of the code must be given in the Data Availability Statement (DAS) at acceptance; if full code cannot be released, pseudocode must be deposited and a binary provided to editors/reviewers; if data are restricted, a representative public dataset supporting similar conclusions should be provided. Custom code must be accompanied by the complete training and testing dataset (excerpt wording).
- Data/code review: https://blogs.rsc.org/dd/data-reviewer/ (fetched and read): for manuscripts with original research "an additional data reviewer will also assess and comment on the data and code provided"; reviewers must be able to access code and data during peer review; materials are made public at publication; RSC aims at FAIR data. (A 2021 blog post; check currency.) Related: https://rsc.org/journals-books-databases/author-and-reviewer-hub/authors-information/insights/2023/july/open-data-in-digital-discovery and the open-data article "Journal research data policies in materials science" (https://pubs.rsc.org/dd/article/5/7/2795/1262769/Journal-research-data-policies-in-materials).
- Implication for this manuscript: a DAS is needed at submission; the PDP export (43,398 records), the LLM-extracted JSON features, prompts/schema, the material-property table (including provenance and Materials Project licensing), and the exact split DOIs should be deposited (Zenodo) with versions; reviewer access to the repository must work during review (private Zenodo/GitHub link or anonymised repository).
- AI-tool disclosure: I could NOT retrieve RSC's official AI policy text. Search returned only a third-party aggregator and unrelated publishers; the aggregator says RSC prohibits AI authorship and requires disclosure of AI use in the Experimental or Acknowledgements section. Treat as UNVERIFIED and read the RSC "Author and reviewer hub" ethics/AI policy directly. Practical note: the LLM used for data extraction is part of the method and must be fully described (model name/version, prompts, schema, validation); any AI assistance in writing should be disclosed per RSC wording once confirmed.
